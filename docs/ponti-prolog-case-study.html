01 / The problem

The valuable information was already there. It was just trapped in email.

Every submission contained useful structured information. But people still had to open the message, interpret it, pull out the important details, and enter them somewhere else.

That kind of workflow looks harmless when volume is low. At scale, it becomes a tax on every person touching the system: repetitive data entry, inconsistent records, slower decisions, and a growing archive that becomes harder to search precisely when it becomes more valuable.

The opportunity wasn’t “add AI.” It was simpler: stop forcing humans to translate information the software could understand itself.

02 / The system

Read it. Structure it. Make it searchable.

We designed the workflow around the thing people were already doing: receiving submissions. No new ritual. No extra form. No second system to feed.

Incoming emails were parsed with an LLM to identify and structure the useful information automatically. That structured data then became part of a searchable candidate corpus, with vector search allowing people to find relevant submissions based on meaning instead of exact keyword matches.

03 / The product thinking

The clever part wasn’t the model.

The model could extract information. The product problem was deciding what should happen around it: what needed structure, what should remain flexible, how a human would recover when extraction was imperfect, and how the resulting information would actually become useful downstream.

Before

Humans acted as middleware.

Read the email. Understand the submission. Copy the facts. Normalize them mentally. Enter them again. Remember where everything lives. Repeat.

After

The system does the clerical work.

People stay focused on judgment and selection while software handles extraction, organization, and retrieval in the background.

04 / The outcome

A smaller workflow. A more useful dataset.

The immediate win was removing repetitive work. The compounding win was turning every submission into structured institutional knowledge that became easier to retrieve as the system grew.
